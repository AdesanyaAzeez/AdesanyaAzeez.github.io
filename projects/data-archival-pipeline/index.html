Data Archival
Pipeline

A hot-to-cold archival workflow to move historical data from operational databases to durable object storage — shrinking the production footprint, shortening backup and restore times, and materially reducing storage and backup costs.

• Python • Amazon S3 • Data Engineering
Role Data Archival Engineer
Area Hot / Cold tiering · Storage Optimization

Context

Several operational databases had accumulated years of high-volume transactional data, increasing the storage footprint of production systems. Much of the historical data was no longer needed for day-to-day operational workloads but still occupied primary database storage.

The organisation needed a disciplined hot-to-cold archival programme: move aged rows out of production storage into cheap, durable object storage — with a verifiable, auditable, and practiced restore playbook so nobody felt uncomfortable deleting rows after archiving them.

Problem

Keeping all history on hot operational storage created four compounding problems:

  • Storage & backup cost. Linear cost growth that was going to get materially worse over the next year.
  • Backup & restore windows. Full backups were taking hours; restores were operationally painful and slow.
  • OLTP performance. The largest historical tables pulled cache and IO budget away from recent, customer-impacting queries.
  • Trust & risk. No one would sign off deleting aged rows because there was no retrieval path with proof of correctness.

Approach

The archival strategy was designed around a simple principle: move cold data safely, verify its availability in the analytical warehouse, archive it to low-cost object storage, and only then remove it from the operational database.

  1. Identify cold data. Retention rules were applied to identify records that had aged beyond the operational retention window and were no longer required in the hot transactional database.
  2. Extract from the operational database. Cold records were extracted in controlled batches using the appropriate archival keys. Batching kept the workload predictable and reduced the impact on the operational database.
  3. Verify warehouse availability. Before removing any records from the hot database, the corresponding data was verified against the analytical warehouse to confirm that the required historical records were already available downstream.
  4. Archive to Amazon S3. Verified historical data was exported as CSV files and stored in Amazon S3 as the cold-storage layer, providing durable and cost-efficient storage for data that was no longer required in the operational database.
  5. Remove from hot storage. Deletion from the operational database was performed only after the data had been verified in the warehouse and successfully written to S3.

This established a controlled hot → warehouse → cold lifecycle, reducing the operational storage footprint while preserving historical data for analytics and future retrieval.

Hot → Warehouse → Cold lifecycle

The archival workflow connected the operational database, analytical warehouse, and S3 cold-storage layer:

  • Identify & extract: Python-based archival processes identified records outside the hot-data retention window and extracted them in manageable batches.
  • Warehouse verification: Each archival batch was checked against the analytical warehouse before any destructive operation was performed.
  • Cold storage: Verified records were exported as CSV and written to Amazon S3, providing a durable and lower-cost storage layer for historical data.
  • Hot-tier cleanup: Once the archival and verification steps completed successfully, the corresponding records were removed from the operational database.
  • Controlled execution: The process was designed to run incrementally, allowing historical data to be archived progressively rather than through a single large migration.

Key Engineering Challenges

  • Delete safety. Nothing was allowed to delete from hot unless verification independently confirmed correctness. The verify pass was written by a separate code path than the extract path, so a bug in extract cannot silently win.
  • Schema evolution & cold retrievability. Immutable Parquet batches should remain readable years later. Versioned schema snapshots per run plus a forward-compatible Parquet schema contract guarantee this.
  • Retention compliance. A retention metadata layer captures the legal/compliance retention class for every batch so lifecycle policies and audit queries are both simple and auditable.
  • Restore performance. Cold data had to be usable without a full re-load into the warehouse. Columnar Parquet + partitioning meant ad-hoc query engines could query S3 directly for most restore/audit use cases with good performance.

Outcome & Impact

~40%

Reduction in operational database footprint by progressively moving aged historical data out of hot storage.

Lower storage overhead

Historical data was moved from the operational database to Amazon S3, reducing the volume of data retained in the higher-cost hot tier.

Improved operational efficiency

Keeping only relevant, recent data in the transactional database reduced the amount of data that needed to be maintained within the operational environment.

Preserved historical data

Archived records remained available through the analytical warehouse and S3, ensuring historical data could continue to support reporting, analytics, and future retrieval without remaining in the hot database.

The result was a more sustainable hot-to-cold data lifecycle: recent transactional data remained readily available in the operational database, while older data was progressively archived to lower-cost storage without losing its analytical value.
